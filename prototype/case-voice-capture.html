Voice capture for Hinglish: testing whether to build

Problem statement

Phone speech-to-text works for American English but breaks on Indian accents and on Hinglish, the Hindi-English mix many Indians speak every day.

Skip to the decisions

Problem

Built-in recognition gets regional accents and Hinglish wrong. I saw it first-hand transcribing my own long Hindi-English recordings.

Not built yet. A pre-build study of four speech engines, scored on real recordings, to decide whether a paid product is worth building.

Problem

Phone speech-to-text is built for American English and breaks for many Indian speakers

  • Regional accents and dialects produce wrong words and broken sentences.
  • Indians often mix Hindi and English in one sentence. Built-in engines handle this badly.
  • I saw this first-hand: built-in phone recognition struggled with local accents, and transcribing my own long Hindi-English recordings took real effort.

Users

Students are easy to test with, but sales and client-facing professionals are who would pay

SegmentUse caseWillingness to pay
Students (for testing)Record lectures, get notes and summariesLow to medium; easy to reach
Sales and client-facing professionalsCalls and meetings in mixed languageHigh. Globally, sales teams bring most AI notetaker revenue (Sacra on Otter.ai)
Field workers and SMB ownersVoice notes instead of typingMedium; large but harder to reach

Hypotheses

Three hypotheses decide whether this gets built

  • H1. The gap is in Hinglish and regional accents, not Indian English overall. A general engine can look fine on average and still fail the users who need it most.
  • H2. An India-first engine closes the gap. At least one engine cuts Hinglish word errors by 25% or more against Apple's free baseline.
  • H3. The economics work for one paying segment. A heavy user's monthly audio cost is under 40% of a plan price that segment says it would pay.

Research plan

The plan: interview buyers, and run four engines on the same real clips

  1. Buyer interviewsSales and client-facing professionals, the segment most likely to pay: how they capture calls today, where voice fails them, and what they pay for now. Students answer the same workflow questions, but their answers are not evidence of willingness to pay.
  2. Accent test20 to 30 real clips (lectures, client calls, Hindi-English mixes) from speakers across India, plus open Indian-accent data such as AI4Bharat's Svarah set.
  3. Engines comparedSarvam (built for Indian languages), OpenAI's mini transcription model, Deepgram Nova-3 (Hinglish mode), and Apple's built-in engine as the free baseline.
  4. MetricWord error rate (WER) by accent and by language mix, plus speed and cost per hour.

Decision rule

The rule, set before the first clip: build, pivot or kill on one number

If the data showsThen
The best engine cuts Hinglish WER by 25% or more against Apple's baseline, and its heavy-user cost is under 40% of a plan price the paying segment acceptsBuild a narrow pilot for that segment
The best engine improves Hinglish WER by less than 25%Pivot: compete on output (summaries, action items) on top of free transcripts
Every engine, including the best, gets more than 1 word in 4 wrong on HinglishKill for now: correcting the transcript takes longer than typing notes. Revisit when models improve

If the accuracy bar is met but the cost bar is not, the answer is a pivot to capped plans, not a build.

Unit economics

A heavy student costs about Rs 660 a month, which points the price at sales teams

One 1-hour lecture a day for a month (about 22 hours) costs about Rs 660 on Sarvam or about $4 on OpenAI's mini model at list prices.

To keep audio under 40% of revenue, that heavy user's plan would need to cost about Rs 1,650 a month on Sarvam, or about $10 on OpenAI's mini model. Students rate their willingness to pay low to medium, so heavy student use cannot be profitable without an hours cap. Prices at that level point to sales and client-facing professionals, which is why the buyer interviews go to them.

Decisions and trade-offs

Four calls: test first, pick the accent winner, price by hours, compete above the transcript

01Test before build

What I chose Chosen

1 to 2 weeks of evaluation first

What I gave up

Speed to a demo

Why. Choosing an engine by assumption risks building on the wrong base.

02Cloud engine over on-device

What I chose Chosen

Pick the winner of the accent test; keep Apple's engine as an offline backup

What I gave up

Free, private, offline-first

Why. Accuracy on local accents is the whole product.

03Pricing model

What I chose Chosen

Monthly plans with an hours allowance

What I gave up

A simple one-time price

Why. Every hour of audio costs money. A one-time price loses money on heavy users.

04Differentiation

What I chose Chosen

Win on output: summaries, action items, exports for one user group

What I gave up

Competing on raw transcription

Why. Phone makers keep improving free recorders. Raw transcripts will become a commodity.
