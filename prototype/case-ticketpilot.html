TicketPilot: an AI router that failed its own test

Problem statement

In IT support, tickets get passed between teams and employees wait while each team repeats the investigation.

Skip to the decisions

Problem

In a real public IT-support log, half of incidents pass through more than one team and 15.6% bounce straight back to a team that already had them.

Product

Reads a new ticket, proposes the resolving team and shows matched past fixes with sources. An engineer approves the route; the employee confirms the outcome.

North Star

Durable first-owner resolution rate (proposed): resolved on time by the first owner, confirmed by the employee, not reopened in 7 days.

Result

The prototype failed its test: 10% first-time-right against a keyword rule's 90%. Recommendation: hold deployment, fund bounded discovery.

Problem

Half of IT incidents change teams, and about 1 in 6 bounce straight back

The pattern comes from one public incident log (BPI Challenge 2013). It shows where friction exists. It does not show why, or that it can be removed.

  • 7,554 incidents and 9,454 handoffs between support teams: 1.25 handoffs per incident on average.
  • 50.6% of incidents touch more than one team.
  • 15.6% bounce directly from team A to B and back to A.
  • Hypothesis: some bounces happen because the ticket's wording points to a different team than the one that ends up resolving it, and past resolutions could predict the right owner.

Many transfers may be legitimate specialist escalations. Finding out how many is the first job of any real pilot.

Key insight

Key insight

A convincing demo does not overturn a failed quality gate.

The prototype looks good on a hand-picked ticket. Across 50 held-out descriptions it did not beat a simple keyword rule. I kept the failed result and built the recommendation around it.

Solution

The product: AI proposes the owner, a person approves, the employee confirms

AI classifies, a person decidesTicketPilot proposes a team from matched past resolutions. Weak evidence abstains. The engineer approves or overrides.
Fixes come with sourcesSuggestions are retrieved from past resolution notes and current articles, not generated, so each can be inspected.
Suggested is not doneA suggested fix, recorded work and the employee's confirmation are three separate steps.

Early feedback said it felt like a test summary and that people could not tell who does what. I changed it to lead with one request journey, named roles and a guided tour. That was feedback on the project, not customer interviews.

Decisions and trade-offs

Four calls that kept the AI honest

01Inspectable retrieval, not an agent stack

What I chose Chosen

Direct retrieval and voting over similar past tickets

What I gave up

The LLM agent stack in my original plan

Why. Behaviour I can inspect, at low setup cost. It also means I cannot claim an LLM agent was built: it was not.

02A person approves the route

What I chose Chosen

TicketPilot classifies; the engineer approves or overrides

What I gave up

Fully automatic assignment

Why. A wrong confident route costs more than a slower right one. Weak evidence abstains to a person.

03Keep the failed result

What I chose Chosen

Report 10% against 90% and recommend HOLD

What I gave up

A cleaner headline

Why. Hiding the abstentions or the baseline would make the demo look better and the decision worse.

04Count effort, not waiting

What I chose Chosen

Value only hands-on minutes, and count review as a cost

What I gave up

The bigger number from elapsed delay

Why. Hours a ticket waits are not hours someone worked. My first formula multiplied delay by volume; I replaced it.

Evaluation

The test failed: 10% first-time-right against a 90% keyword baseline

MeasureResultHow to read it
Keyword baseline, first-time-right45 of 50 (90%)Frozen rules on the same descriptions
TicketPilot, first-time-right5 of 50 (10%)Abstentions count as not assigned
Recommendation accuracy44 of 50 (88%)Includes the team proposed on cases it held back
Sent to human review45 of 50 (90%)Little coverage, and a real review cost
Wrong confident assignments0 of 5Too few to show a low error rate: zero errors in 5 still allows about 45% at 95% confidence

The 50 test cases are five variants of ten authored scenarios, so they are synthetic and correlated. This is a failed comparison against a baseline, not a measure of real-world accuracy. The gate also needs review: beating a 90% baseline by 10 points requires 100%. That review has to happen before the next experiment, not after seeing its results.

Value case

Even if it worked, review effort decides whether the economics hold

Base case, with every input assumed: 20,000 tickets a year, 25% fewer handoffs, 10 hands-on minutes per handoff, $45 an hour, 20% of tickets reviewed for 5 minutes, $12,000 to build and $6,600 a year to run. That gives about 710 net hours and $25,332 a year, with payback in 5.7 months.

Recommendation

So: hold the router and fund 90 days of gated discovery

Authorise bounded discovery within an illustrative $12,000 build budget and hold deployment of the current router. Each stage is released only when its gate passes. These are my proposals, not approvals from anyone.

StageWorkExit gate
Days 1 to 30: discovery and offline qualityValidate the transfers and labels, interview users, measure effort, test on approved real ticket textNo live assignment. Pass the quality, review and safety gates, or rework or stop
Days 31 to 60: one team, controlledShadow mode first, then human-approved routing on one registered cohortFewer handoffs, no worse p90 time, 60% of resolvers active weekly for two weeks
Days 61 to 90: value validationMature the outcome cohorts; Finance validates effort and costExpand only with passing quality, adoption and safety, and payback within 12 months

What I would do differently: compare against the cheap alternative first, get real ticket text earlier, and set the gate and the baseline together. I wrote a gate that demands 100% before I knew the baseline.
